3.1 Why Data Transformation Matters

Insurance data are rarely collected in exactly the form needed for analysis. A dataset may contain individual policy records, claim records, exposure measures, and characteristics of policyholders or insured objects. Before these data can be used to answer an actuarial question, they often need to be transformed, organized, and summarized.

Data transformation refers to changing the structure or representation of data so that they are suitable for a particular analysis. This may involve creating new variables, changing the units of measurement, grouping observations, filtering records, or combining information from different variables.

For example, consider a motor insurance dataset containing the policyholder’s age, ZIP code, number of claims, exposure, and aggregate claim amount. The raw variables may be sufficient for storing the data, but they do not immediately provide measures such as claim frequency or average claim severity. These measures must be calculated from the underlying data.

\[ \text{Raw data} \longrightarrow \text{Transformed data} \longrightarrow \text{Summary} \longrightarrow \text{Actuarial insight}. \]

The purpose of transformation is therefore not simply to make the dataset look cleaner. It is to create variables and summaries that help answer a specific insurance question.

For example, an insurer may want to investigate whether claim experience differs by age group. The original dataset may contain the exact age of each policyholder, but an analysis may require age groups such as 18–25, 26–35, 36–45, and so on. Similarly, comparing the number of claims between two groups without considering exposure can be misleading. A group with more policies will generally have more claims simply because it has more opportunities for claims to occur.

This is why actuarial analysis often transforms the original data into measures that account for the volume of business. For example, claim frequency can be calculated as

\[ \text{Claim Frequency} =\frac{\text{Number of Claims}} {\text{Exposure}}, \]

while average claim severity can be calculated as

\[ \text{Average Severity} =\frac{\text{Aggregate Claim Amount}} {\text{Number of Claims}}. \]

These quantities provide more meaningful information about the underlying risk than claim counts or aggregate claim amounts alone.

NoteTransformation depends on the question

There is no single transformation that is appropriate for every analysis. The same raw dataset may be transformed in different ways depending on the actuarial question being investigated.

For example, age may be used as an exact numerical variable when studying how risk changes with age, or it may be grouped into age bands when constructing an easily interpretable rating structure.

Data summarization is the next step. A large dataset may contain thousands of individual observations, making it difficult to identify patterns by inspecting individual records. Summaries condense the data into a smaller set of informative quantities, such as counts, totals, averages, medians, and measures of variability.

In insurance, summaries are often produced separately for different risk groups. For example, an insurer might compare exposure, claim frequency, and average severity across ZIP codes:

ZIP Exposure Claims Claim Frequency Average Severity
0 206.84 29 0.140 28,328
1 11,080.63 1,593 0.144 72,931
2 7,782.63 1,008 0.130 59,278
3 7,587.56 1,038 0.137 56,829

Such a table is much easier to interpret than the underlying individual policy records. It allows an analyst to compare both risk experience and portfolio volume across groups.

However, a summary is not itself an explanation. Differences between groups may arise for many reasons, including differences in the underlying risk characteristics, random variation, or differences in the composition of the portfolio. Data transformation and summarization therefore provide the evidence needed for further analysis; they do not replace actuarial judgement.

The central idea of this chapter is therefore:

\[ \boxed{ \text{Transform the data to represent the question, then summarize it to reveal the evidence.} } \]

These skills form an essential foundation for exploratory data analysis, statistical modelling, and insurance rating. Before fitting a model, an actuary should understand what the data contain, how the variables have been constructed, and what the basic summaries reveal about the portfolio.

3.2 Inspecting Data

Before transforming or summarizing a dataset, we first need to understand its structure and the meaning of the information it contains. This initial inspection is an important part of the data-science workflow because subsequent calculations are meaningful only when the underlying data have been correctly understood.

A dataset consists of observations and variables. An observation represents the unit being studied, while a variable records a characteristic of that unit. In insurance data, an observation might represent a policy, a policy-year, or another defined unit of exposure. Variables may describe the policyholder, the insured object, the exposure, or the claims arising during the observation period.

For example, a claims experience dataset may contain variables such as:

Variable Description Possible type
age_policyholder Age of the policyholder Numeric
nclaims Number of claims Numeric
exposure Amount of exposure during the observation period Numeric
amount Aggregate claim amount Numeric
power Vehicle power Numeric
bm Bonus-malus level Categorical or ordinal
zip Geographic area Categorical

Some of these variables describe the outcome of insurance experience, while others describe characteristics that may be associated with that experience. In actuarial applications, variables that may be used to differentiate risks for rating purposes are commonly called rating variables. They may include characteristics such as age, geographic area, vehicle characteristics, or previous claims experience.

3.2.1 Observations and variables

The number of observations is one of the first things we should establish. It tells us how many units are represented in the dataset and provides context for all subsequent calculations.

The number of observations, however, is not necessarily the same as the amount of insurance business represented by the data. In insurance, exposure provides an important measure of portfolio volume. For example, two groups may contain the same number of policy records but represent different amounts of exposure because policies may have been in force for different lengths of time.

This distinction becomes particularly important when comparing claims experience. A group with more claims does not necessarily have a higher underlying claim risk if it also has substantially greater exposure.

3.2.2 Variable types

Before performing calculations, each variable should be classified according to its meaning.

A numeric variable represents a quantity for which numerical operations are meaningful. Examples include age, exposure, number of claims, and claim amount. Such variables can be summarized using quantities such as means, medians, minima, maxima, and standard deviations.

A categorical variable represents membership in a group or category. Examples include vehicle type, geographic area, and rating class.

A variable should be classified according to its meaning, rather than simply according to the way its values are stored. For example, a ZIP code may be recorded using numbers such as 0, 1, 2, and 3, but these numbers identify geographic groups rather than quantities. Calculating their arithmetic mean would therefore have no useful interpretation.

ImportantNumeric values are not necessarily numeric variables

The fact that a variable contains numbers does not mean that arithmetic operations on those numbers are meaningful.

For example, if zip takes the values 0, 1, 2, and 3, it should normally be treated as a categorical variable. The value 3 does not represent three units of geographic area, nor is the average ZIP code an actuarial quantity of interest.

Some variables may also have an ordinal interpretation. For example, a bonus-malus level may consist of ordered categories in which higher or lower levels indicate different positions in a rating system. Although the categories have an order, the numerical codes should not automatically be treated as measurements with equal numerical distances.

3.2.3 Inspecting the values

Variable names and types are not sufficient. We should also examine the values recorded in each variable.

For numeric variables, useful initial questions include:

  • What are the minimum and maximum values?
  • Are there missing values?
  • Are there zero or negative values?
  • Are there unusually large or small observations?
  • What units are being used?

For categorical variables, we should consider:

  • What categories occur?
  • How many observations belong to each category?
  • Are there unexpected categories?
  • Are category labels recorded consistently?

These checks are particularly important for insurance data. For example, a negative claim amount may indicate a data-entry or coding issue, while a zero claim count may be perfectly valid because most policyholders may have no claim during a given observation period.

3.2.4 Tidy data

Once the basic structure of the data has been identified, the next question is whether the data are organized in a form that is convenient for analysis.

A useful standard is tidy data. A dataset is tidy when:

  1. each variable has its own column;
  2. each observation has its own row; and
  3. each value has its own cell.

This structure separates the concepts of observations and variables clearly. It also makes subsequent transformation, summarization, and visualization more systematic.

For example, suppose a claims dataset records the policy number, year, and number of claims. A tidy representation might look like this:

Policy Year Claims
120002 2009 0
120002 2010 1
120003 2009 2
120003 2010 0

Here, Policy, Year, and Claims are variables, while each row represents one policy-year observation. Each value occupies a single cell.

The same information can, however, be stored in several other forms. These representations may be convenient for reporting or data entry, but they are less convenient for data analysis.

Example 1: Variables stored in column names

Consider the following representation:

Policy Claims_2009 Claims_2010
120002 0 1
120003 2 0

The values for Year have been incorporated into the column names. This means that Year is no longer an explicit variable in the dataset.

This structure is often convenient for displaying results, but it becomes less convenient when we want to perform an operation such as:

claims %>%
  group_by(Year) %>%
  summarise(total_claims = sum(Claims))

In the tidy representation, Year is a variable and can therefore be used directly for grouping and summarization. In the representation above, the years must first be extracted from the column names.

This type of transformation is commonly referred to as converting data from wide to long format. In tidyr, the pivot_longer() function is used for this purpose.

Example 2: Multiple variables stored in one column

Suppose claims information is recorded as follows:

Policy Year Information
120002 2009 Claims:0
120002 2010 Claims:1
120003 2009 Claims:2
120003 2010 Claims:0

Here, the column Information contains both the name of the variable (Claims) and its value. The actual claim count is therefore not stored as a separate variable.

A more useful representation is:

Policy Year Claims
120002 2009 0
120002 2010 1
120003 2009 2
120003 2010 0

Separating the variable name from its values makes the data easier to filter, summarize, and visualize.

Example 3: Multiple observations stored in one row

Consider a dataset in which a policy’s claims experience is recorded in a single row:

Policy Claims_1 Claims_2 Claims_3
120002 0 1 0
120003 2 0 1

Suppose Claims_1, Claims_2, and Claims_3 represent claims in three successive years. The year information is again encoded in the column names rather than represented by a separate variable.

A tidy representation would instead have one row for each policy-year observation:

Policy Year Claims
120002 1 0
120002 2 1
120002 3 0
120003 1 2
120003 2 0
120003 3 1

This structure makes it straightforward to perform operations such as calculating total claims by year or comparing claims experience across policies.

The process of converting data from the first representation to the second is another example of transforming wide data into long data.

NoteWhy tidy data matter

Tidy data provide a consistent structure for subsequent operations. Once each observation, variable, and value has a clear place in the dataset, operations such as filtering observations, creating variables, grouping records, and calculating summaries become easier to express and interpret.

Tidy data are not necessarily the only possible way to store data. Wide formats can be useful for reporting and presentation. The purpose of tidy data is to provide a structure that is particularly convenient for data manipulation and analysis.

In practice, data imported from spreadsheets, databases, or other sources may not initially be tidy. A substantial part of data preparation therefore involves reshaping the data into a suitable structure before analysis. The tidyr package provides functions such as pivot_longer() and pivot_wider() for converting between long and wide representations.

3.2.5 From inspection to transformation

Inspecting the data does not mean that we have finished preparing them. Rather, it gives us the information needed to decide what transformation is appropriate.

For example, after inspecting a motor insurance dataset, we might decide to:

  • retain only variables relevant to the analysis;
  • select policies from a particular period;
  • create a claim frequency variable;
  • group policyholders into age bands;
  • classify ZIP codes as rating groups; or
  • calculate claim severity for observations with at least one claim.

These operations correspond to the next stage of the workflow:

\[ \text{Inspect} \longrightarrow \text{Transform} \longrightarrow \text{Summarize}. \]

In R, the dplyr package provides a set of functions designed for these common operations. The most important functions introduced in this chapter are select(), filter(), mutate(), group_by(), and summarise().

NoteFurther reference: dplyr

The dplyr package provides a consistent set of tools for transforming and summarizing data in R. This chapter introduces its core functions for common data-manipulation tasks.

For the complete reference and additional examples, see the official dplyr documentation.

3.4 Selecting and Filtering Data

Once a dataset has been inspected and organized in a tidy form, the next step is to work with the observations and variables relevant to the question being investigated. A dataset may contain many variables and observations, but an analysis rarely requires all of them at once.

Two common operations are therefore selecting variables and filtering observations. In dplyr, these operations are performed using select() and filter().

3.4.1 Selecting variables

The select() function is used to choose particular variables from a data frame. This is useful when a dataset contains many columns but only a subset is relevant to the current analysis.

For example, suppose a claims dataset contains information on policy number, year, coverage, claim frequency, and several other variables. We may select only the variables needed to examine claims experience:

3.4.5 Why selection and filtering matter in actuarial analysis

Selection and filtering are not merely programming operations. They determine which part of the portfolio is being analyzed.

For example, an analyst might investigate:

  • policies written during a particular year;
  • policyholders within a particular age range;
  • policies belonging to a particular ZIP code;
  • observations with positive claims;
  • a particular set of rating classes.

The resulting summaries describe only the observations that have been retained. Therefore, the filtering criteria should always be stated clearly when reporting an analysis.

This is especially important when analyzing claims. If zero-claim observations are removed before calculating average severity, the resulting statistic describes the severity conditional on a claim occurring. It does not describe the average claim amount across all policyholders.

ImportantAlways ask: what observations are being analyzed?

A summary statistic is meaningful only when we know which observations were included in its calculation.

For example,

\[ \frac{\text{Aggregate Claim Amount}} {\text{Number of Claims}} \]

describes average claim severity, whereas excluding observations with zero claims before calculating this quantity does not mean that zero claims have been ignored from the underlying portfolio; they simply do not contribute to the denominator because they contain no claim whose severity can be measured.

Selection and filtering therefore provide the basic tools for defining the scope of an analysis. Once the relevant observations and variables have been identified, the next step is often to create new variables that express quantities more directly relevant to the actuarial question.

3.5 Creating and Transforming Variables

After selecting the relevant observations and variables, the next step is often to create variables that are more useful for the analysis. A raw dataset may contain the information needed to calculate an actuarial quantity, but that quantity may not yet exist as a variable in the dataset.

For example, a claims dataset may contain the total claim amount and the number of claims, but not the average claim severity. We can create a new variable from the existing variables:

\[ \text{Average Severity} =\frac{\text{Aggregate Claim Amount}} {\text{Number of Claims}}. \]

In dplyr, the mutate() function is used to create new variables or transform existing ones. The basic form is:

  mutate(new_variable = expression)

3.5.5 Transforming variables for analysis

Variable transformation can also make the data easier to analyze. A continuous variable may, for example, be converted into groups when the analysis requires comparisons between categories.

3.6 Combining Operations with Pipes

Data transformation often involves several operations performed in sequence. For example, we may first select the variables of interest, then filter the observations, and finally create a new variable. 256 %>% sqrt()

sqrt(256)
[1] 16

256 %>% sqrt() %>% log2()

3.7 Grouping and Summarizing Data

A large dataset is often too detailed to interpret by inspecting individual observations. Summary statistics provide a way to reduce many observations to a small number of quantities that describe important features of the data.

Common summary statistics include the mean, standard deviation, minimum, maximum, median, and quantiles. In actuarial analysis, other summaries such as total exposure, number of claims, aggregate claim amount, claim frequency, and average claim severity are also important.

The dplyr package provides the summarise() function for calculating summary statistics.

For example, suppose we want to calculate the mean and standard deviation of average claim severity for observations in 2010 where at least one claim occurred:

Code 
summary_table <- claims %>%
  filter(Year == 2010 & Freq > 0) %>%
  summarise(
    Mean = mean(yAvg),
    SD = sd(yAvg)
  )

The resulting data frame contains one row with the two requested summaries. The names Mean and SD are chosen by the analyst.

The filtering step is important here. Average claim severity is meaningful only for observations with positive claim counts. The source material uses the same sequence of filtering to calculate the mean and standard deviation of average severity for 2010.

3.7.1 Grouping observations

A single summary for the entire dataset may hide important differences between groups. In exploratory analysis, we often want to calculate the same summaries separately for different levels of a variable.

The group_by() function divides the data into groups. A subsequent summarise() then calculates the requested summaries separately within each group.

For example, instead of calculating average severity for only 2010, we can calculate it separately for each year:

Code 
summary_table_all_years <- claims %>%
  group_by(Year) %>%
  filter(Freq > 0) %>%
  summarise(
    Mean = mean(yAvg),
    SD = sd(yAvg)
  )

The result contains one row for each year, with the mean and standard deviation calculated using the observations belonging to that year. The source material demonstrates this use of group_by(Year) followed by summarise().

The conceptual difference is:

summarise(...)

calculates a summary for the selected data as a whole, whereas

group_by(variable) %>%
  summarise(...)

calculates the summary separately for each level of variable.

This distinction is fundamental to actuarial analysis because risk experience is rarely homogeneous across an entire portfolio.

Grouping becomes particularly useful when investigating rating variables. For example, we may want to compare claims experience across characteristics such as entity type, alarm credit, no-claim credit, or fire class. 3.8 Insurance Summaries

The grouping and summarizing operations introduced above allow us to move from individual observations to quantities that are more directly relevant to actuarial analysis. In insurance, we are often interested in the number and cost of claims and in measures that allow claims experience to be compared across groups.

Several basic quantities are particularly important.

3.8.1 Claim count

The claim count is the total number of claims observed within a group. In the current dataset, Freq represents the number of claims for each observation. We can therefore calculate the total claim count for each EntityType as follows:

Code 
claims %>%
  group_by(EntityType) %>%
  summarise(

2 County       1607
3 Misc          113
4 School       2290
5 Town          100
6 Village       606

The resulting value represents the total number of claims observed in each group.

3.8.2 Aggregate claim amount

The aggregate claim amount is the total amount paid or incurred for claims within a group. If y represents the aggregate claim amount for each observation, it can be obtained by summing y:

Code 
claims %>%
  group_by(EntityType) %>%
  summarise(
    claims = sum(Freq),
    amount = sum(y)
  )
# A tibble: 6 × 3
  EntityType claims    amount

2 County       1607 25491545.
3 Misc          113  3401011.
4 School       2290 42999241.
5 Town          100  1605103.
6 Village       606  5376997.

The claim count and aggregate claim amount describe two different aspects of claims experience. A group may have many claims but relatively low total claim costs, or relatively few claims but a high total claim amount.

3.8.3 Claim severity

Claim severity measures the average amount associated with a claim. At the group level, it can be calculated as aggregate claim amount divided by the total number of claims:

\[ \text{Severity}_j =\frac{\text{Aggregate claims}_j} {\text{Claims}_j}, \]

where \(j\) denotes a particular rating group.

For example:

Code 
claims %>%
  group_by(EntityType) %>%
  summarise(
    claims = sum(Freq),
    amount = sum(y),
    severity = amount / claims
  )

4 School       2290 42999241.   18777.

This calculation gives the average claim amount for each EntityType. It is preferable to simply taking the average of individual claim amounts when observations may represent different numbers of claims, because the group-level calculation uses the total claim amount and total number of claims.

3.8.4 Exposure and claim frequency

In many insurance applications, claim counts are interpreted relative to the amount of exposure underlying the business. Exposure measures the amount of risk insured or the period for which the risk was covered. For example, one policy-year may represent one unit of exposure.

When exposure is available, the claim frequency for group \(j\) can be calculated as

\[ \text{Frequency}_j =\frac{\text{Claims}_j} {\text{Exposure}_j}. \]

This is different from claim count. A group with 100 claims does not necessarily have a higher claim frequency than a group with 50 claims if the first group has substantially more exposure.

The current claims dataset does not contain an exposure variable. Therefore, we do not calculate exposure-based claim frequency from this dataset. Instead, we focus on claim counts, aggregate claim amounts, and severity. Exposure-based frequency will be introduced when we work with a dataset containing an appropriate exposure measure.

3.8.5 Frequency, severity, and risk premium

In actuarial analysis, frequency and severity are often combined to describe the expected claim cost per unit of exposure. Conceptually,

\[ \text{Risk premium} =\text{Frequency} \times \text{Severity}. \]

Thus, when exposure is available,

\[ \text{Risk premium}_j =\frac{\text{Claims}_j} {\text{Exposure}_j} \times \frac{\text{Aggregate claims}_j} {\text{Claims}_j} =\frac{\text{Aggregate claims}_j} {\text{Exposure}_j}. \]

This relationship provides an important connection between the basic summaries and later actuarial pricing models. Frequency describes how often claims occur, severity describes the average cost of a claim, and their product gives the expected claim cost per unit of exposure.

For the current dataset, however, the quantities that can be directly summarized are claim count, aggregate claim amount, and severity:

When exposure is available, the basic relationships can be summarized as

\[ \text{Frequency} =\frac{\text{Claims}}{\text{Exposure}}, \]

\[ \text{Severity} =\frac{\text{Aggregate claims}}{\text{Claims}}, \]

\[ \text{Risk premium} =\text{Frequency}\times\text{Severity}. \]

\[ \text{Risk premium} =\frac{\text{Aggregate claims}}{\text{Exposure}}. \]

The current dataset allows us to examine claims and severity, but not exposure-based frequency or risk premium.

3.9 Interpreting Insurance Summaries

Calculating insurance summaries is only the first step. In actuarial analysis, the more important question is what these summaries tell us about the underlying claims experience. A difference between two groups does not automatically imply that one group represents a higher-risk class. The size and composition of each group must also be considered.

3.9.1 Comparing groups

Suppose we summarize claims experience by EntityType:

Code 
claims %>%
  group_by(EntityType) %>%

6 Village            1341    606  5376997.    8873.

The resulting table allows us to compare groups using several measures. For example, one group may have a higher aggregate claim amount simply because it contains more observations. Another group may have fewer claims but a substantially higher average severity.

It is therefore useful to consider experience and volume together.

3.9.2 Experience and volume

The term experience refers to the observed claims results, such as the number or cost of claims. Volume refers to the amount of business supporting those results, such as the number of observations, claims, or exposure.

For example, consider two groups:

Group Observations Claims Aggregate claims Severity
A 1,000 100 500,000 5,000
B 20 5 40,000 8,000

Group B has a higher observed severity, but this result is based on only five claims. Group A has considerably more claims and therefore provides more observations from which to assess the underlying claims experience.

This does not mean that the result for Group B should be ignored. Rather, the difference should be interpreted with appropriate caution.

ImportantVolume matters

A calculated summary should always be considered together with the amount of data supporting it.

A group with a small number of observations or claims can produce an extreme observed result simply through random variation. Conversely, a result based on a large volume of business may provide more stable evidence of an underlying difference in risk.

Therefore, when comparing rating groups, ask both:

  • What is the observed claims experience?
  • How much data support that experience?

3.9.3 Claim count is not frequency

It is also important to distinguish claim count from claim frequency.

A claim count measures the number of claims:

\[ \text{Claims}*j = \sum_i N*{ij}. \]

A claim frequency relates the number of claims to the amount of exposure:

\[ \text{Frequency}_j =\frac{\text{Claims}_j}{\text{Exposure}_j}. \]

Consequently, comparing claim counts between groups can be misleading when the groups contain different amounts of business.

For example, a group with 200 claims may appear to have worse experience than a group with 100 claims. However, if the first group has 10,000 exposure units while the second has only 1,000, their claim frequencies are

\[ \frac{200}{10{,}000}=0.02 \]

and

\[ \frac{100}{1{,}000}=0.10, \]

respectively. The group with fewer claims actually has the higher claim frequency.

3.9.4 Observed differences are not necessarily causal

Differences between rating groups should also be interpreted carefully. A group may have higher observed severity because of another characteristic that is associated with both the group and claim severity.

For example, suppose one EntityType has higher observed claim severity. This does not by itself establish that the type of entity causes higher claim severity. The group may differ from other groups in coverage, deductible, location, or other risk characteristics.

This is one reason why simple grouped summaries are useful for exploratory analysis, but are not sufficient on their own for determining rating relativities or premiums.

3.9.5 From summaries to actuarial judgment

Grouped summaries provide an initial view of the claims experience associated with different rating variables. They help identify patterns, unusual observations, and potential differences between groups.

However, actuarial analysis requires more than identifying which group has the largest observed value. A sound interpretation considers:

  1. the observed claims experience;
  2. the volume of business supporting the experience;
  3. the definition of the measure being compared;
  4. other characteristics that may differ between groups; and
  5. the uncertainty associated with the observed results.

These considerations provide the bridge from descriptive analysis to actuarial modelling. Statistical models can then be used to assess the relationship between claims outcomes and multiple rating variables simultaneously, rather than relying only on unadjusted comparisons between groups.

NoteFrom data to actuarial decision

The purpose of data manipulation and summarization is not simply to produce tables. The objective is to obtain evidence that can support actuarial judgment.

A useful progression is:

\[ \text{Data} \rightarrow \text{Summary} \rightarrow \text{Comparison} \rightarrow \text{Interpretation} \rightarrow \text{Actuarial decision}. \]

The summaries developed in this chapter provide the descriptive foundation for the statistical and actuarial models considered later.
